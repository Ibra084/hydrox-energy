How Stage 1 would work

Stage 1: an existing-reactor feasibility pathway.

Hydrox models a hypothetical split of one unit's electricity between the grid and a high-temperature electrolyser, and asks when that split creates more value than exporting the power.

Step 1 · 1,337 MWe assumed unit rating

Electricity is allocated

The model allocates part of one unit's output, such as 310 MWe, to hydrogen and the rest to the grid. Hydrox is designed to remain outside the reactor protection and safety-control systems; any future integration would need independently engineered interfaces, approved operating constraints and regulatory assessment.

Step 2 · ~800 °C inside the stack

Steam is split

High-temperature steam electrolysis driven by nuclear electricity, with optional low-grade steam integration. A pressurised-water reactor does not deliver the ~800 °C used inside an SOEC stack, so electrical heating and recuperation supply the rest. The thermal integration and its electricity-generation penalty must be quantified.

Step 3 · H₂

Hydrogen is delivered

Product boundary: hydrogen at ~20–30 bar at the plant gate. Further compression, storage, transport and conversion to ammonia sit outside the modelled boundary.

Next · Stages 2 and 3

One framework, pathway-specific models.

Future high-temperature gas-cooled reactors may supply higher-grade heat through intermediate heat exchangers. Thermochemical water splitting is a longer-term research pathway. Each needs its own process model and constraints.

SHEET 02 / QUESTION
The research question

Under what electricity prices, hydrogen values, equipment constraints and emissions conditions does diverting nuclear-generated energy to high-temperature electrolysis outperform exporting the same energy to the grid?

The question isn't whether nuclear power can produce hydrogen. It's when producing hydrogen creates more value than supplying electricity. Hydrox combines hydrogen process modelling, electricity opportunity-cost analysis, operating constraints and emissions accounting to find out.

Outcome 1

Hydrogen wins on both

Producing hydrogen is economically and environmentally preferable to exporting the electricity.

Outcome 2

Profitable, but higher emissions

Hydrogen pays more, but the diverted electricity is replaced by fossil generation and system emissions rise.

Outcome 3

Lower emissions, but uneconomic

Hydrogen reduces emissions, but earns less than selling the electricity.

Outcome 4

Export wins on both

Sending the electricity to the grid is better on economic and emissions grounds.

Hydrox is designed to identify both the conditions under which nuclear hydrogen makes sense and the conditions under which it does not.

SHEET 03 / PROBLEM
The problem

Firm power helps utilisation. It doesn't make hydrogen cheap on its own.

Low-cost intermittent electricity may reduce an electrolyser's annual utilisation, increasing the capital cost allocated to each kilogram of hydrogen. Firm nuclear electricity can support high utilisation, but its economic advantage depends on the price and opportunity cost of that electricity.

The 3.6× figure is a simplified comparison of 25% and 90% utilisation with identical capital cost, financing, efficiency and operating costs. Real utilisation varies widely with electricity sourcing, contracts and dispatch strategy.

02 · The low-emissions premiumIEA, 2025 data
>100Mt H₂ demand
Low-emissions share
~1%

Global hydrogen demand exceeded 100 million tonnes in 2025, almost all in traditional industry and refining. Low-emissions production grew to almost 1 Mt, still around 1% of output.

Hydrogen is already used extensively in refining and chemical production, but low-emissions alternatives generally face an economic premium relative to conventional fossil-derived supply. Reducing that premium requires improvements in energy costs, capital expenditure, utilisation and operating strategy. Costs, uncertain offtake, infrastructure and regulation remain significant constraints.

SHEET 04 / PATHWAYS
The approach

One extensible optimisation framework across three proposed nuclear-hydrogen pathways.

The three stages require different process models, operating limits and control variables. Hydrox proposes a shared economic optimisation framework, with pathway-specific physical models and constraints. It does not assume one trained model or one unchanged controller works across all three.

Proposed safety architecture

Outside reactor safety systems. Recommendations for the hydrogen plant only.

Hydrox would be restricted to non-safety-related hydrogen-plant operating recommendations, subject to independently implemented constraints. Reactor protection and nuclear safety systems would remain outside Hydrox's authority. Any physical implementation would require formal hazard analysis, verified interfaces, plant-operator approval and applicable regulatory review. No such system exists today.

SHEET 05 / FRAMEWORK
The optimisation framework

What we are building, and how we will test it.

A physics-constrained optimisation framework that evaluates when hydrogen production creates more value than exporting electricity, under safety, economic and emissions constraints. Every component is research-stage and must beat a simple fixed-load baseline to earn its place.

01 · Process model · in development

A physics-based model of the hydrogen plant: electrolysis, heat integration and stack degradation.

02 · Scenario inputs · simulated

Electricity value, hydrogen value and grid-emissions scenarios. Simulated or sample data, not live forecasts.

Hydrox framework
03 · Optimisation · in development

Schedules production against electricity opportunity cost, ramp limits and degradation, benchmarked against fixed-load and rule-based operation.

04 · Constraints · simulation only

Within the simulation, candidate operating decisions are checked against explicitly specified model constraints. No commands are sent to nuclear or industrial equipment.

Far more than electricity. Our cost model has to account for all of these before we publish a Hydrox figure:

  • Electricity price or opportunity cost
  • Thermal input and extraction cost
  • Electrolyser capital expenditure
  • Installation and balance of plant

SHEET 07 / EVIDENCE
Evidence

Research foundations and technology readiness.

These references establish the scientific and technical foundations of the proposed pathways. They do not establish the commercial readiness or performance of an integrated Hydrox system.

Nuclear-powered electrolysis has been demonstrated at an operating nuclear site

001
Nine Mile Point · USA · 2023 · Demonstration scale
Evidence

Constellation began producing hydrogen in March 2023 with an approximately 1.25 MW low-temperature (PEM) electrolyser producing up to approximately 560 kg/day (Constellation).

What it shows

Validates nuclear electricity as a hydrogen energy source. It is not SOEC/HTSE technology and not commercial-scale Barakah deployment.

High-temperature electrolysis performance

002
INL · experimental measurements and engineering studies
Evidence

INL reference HTSE studies report ~36.8 kWh electrical plus 6.4 kWh thermal per kg H₂ (INL). A 2025 study of a 100 kW SOEC system tested at INL measured ~36.7 kWh/kg DC (IJHE).

What it shows

Strong stack and system performance at research scale. The DC figure is not whole-plant consumption, and neither is a nuclear-integrated commercial plant.

Commercial HTGR electricity generation

003
HTR-PM · China · 2023 · Commercial reactor
Evidence

The first modular high-temperature gas-cooled plant entered commercial electricity generation in December 2023, with a ~750 °C helium outlet.

What it shows

Commercial HTGR electricity generation has been demonstrated; integrated HTGR hydrogen production remains a separate engineering and commercialisation challenge.

Thermochemical hydrogen research

004
JAEA · Japan · 2004 · Bench scale
Evidence

About 30 L/h of hydrogen sustained for one week in 2004 on bench-scale sulfur–iodine apparatus (JAEA).

What it shows

Bench-scale experimental demonstration, not commercial nuclear hydrogen production. Corrosion, separation and integration remain open problems.

Barakah-specific techno-economic study

005
APR-1400 · IAEA HEEP model · 2026 · Peer-reviewed modelling
Evidence

Estimated ~$3.39/kg for high-temperature steam electrolysis and ~$4.87/kg for conventional electrolysis integrated with Barakah, against a ~$6.63/kg grid-electricity reference (IJHE).

What it shows

An independent, geographically relevant baseline under that study's assumptions. It is not Hydrox's cost, and UAE solar and wind scenarios can be cheaper.

SHEET 08 / UAE
UAE context

Why the UAE is a useful test case.

Hydrox is independent research. It is not affiliated with, endorsed, funded, approved or operated by ENEC, ADNOC, TA'ZIZ or INL.

01 · Stage 1

Existing reactors, no hydrogen integration

Barakah's four units provide up to 5,600 MW of generating capacity (ENEC). Stage 1 asks whether part of one unit's output could be worth more as hydrogen than as electricity sold to existing consumers. That allocation is hypothetical; Barakah has not agreed to supply Hydrox.

02

Regional industrial hydrogen demand

TA'ZIZ's planned 1 Mtpa ammonia facility (TA'ZIZ, Mitsui) illustrates the scale of hydrogen use in UAE industrial chemicals. Producing that quantity of ammonia requires approximately 176,500 tonnes/year of hydrogen on a stoichiometric basis (3/17 by mass). This does not imply that TA'ZIZ is a Hydrox customer or has uncontracted hydrogen demand.

03

National hydrogen strategy

The UAE National Hydrogen Strategy targets approximately 1.4 Mtpa of hydrogen production by 2031 across specified pathways, and identifies nuclear-derived hydrogen as a potential pathway: it forecasts about 7,500 t/yr from a proposed 50 MW nuclear electricity allocation by 2031. That is a forecast, not achieved production. Hydrox explores nuclear hydrogen within this broader energy-transition context.

Context · January 2022

ENEC previously identified a theoretical potential of approximately 1 million tonnes of nuclear-powered hydrogen annually from Barakah's four units, subject to major electricity allocation and hydrogen infrastructure assumptions.

~1 Mt/yr

Theoretical potential reported by S&P Global, 2022. Not a project, funded investment or production forecast, and it would divert electricity from existing consumers. Hydrox is not part of any ENEC programme.

SHEET 09 / QUESTIONS
Questions engineers ask us

The hard questions, answered plainly.

Why not just sell the electricity?

Often that will be the better choice. Hydrogen production may create higher value under specific conditions, especially where hydrogen has a strong industrial market and the electricity's opportunity cost is sufficiently low. That has to be calculated, not assumed, and it is exactly what Hydrox is built to test.

Why nuclear instead of solar?

Nuclear electricity can support high electrolyser utilisation and reduce dependence on variable generation, but it may carry a higher electricity opportunity cost. Published UAE studies show solar and wind hydrogen can be cheaper under some assumptions, and storage or flexible supply can serve industrial users. Hydrox evaluates the complete economics rather than claiming nuclear is universally superior.

Why is AI needed?

An optimiser may add value when decisions involve many coupled variables, changing forecasts, constraints, nonlinear degradation and competing objectives. But a simple fixed-load or rule-based controller may already perform well. Hydrox has to demonstrate incremental value by benchmarking against those baselines, and will report it if it cannot.

Why would ENEC need Hydrox?

Hydrox is not an ENEC product and has not shown that ENEC needs it. Its relevance is that nuclear-hydrogen integration raises planning and operational optimisation questions worth independent study. The proposed next step is technical validation, not equipment deployment.

What is actually original?

Not nuclear hydrogen, not SOEC chemistry, not sulfur–iodine thermochemistry and not generic model predictive control. The potential contribution is the specific integration of a transparent hydrogen process model, electrical opportunity-cost modelling, degradation-aware dispatch and power-system emissions accounting within a UAE nuclear-hydrogen feasibility framework. It is a research contribution that still requires validation, not a proven breakthrough.

What we are asking for.

Our immediate objective is not to build a reactor-connected hydrogen facility. It is to develop and validate an auditable optimisation model and establish whether the opportunity merits further engineering investigation.

Where we are

Research-stage concept and simulation. No physical deployment. Hydrogen production through electrolysis is technically established, and nuclear-powered electrolysis has already been demonstrated. High-temperature steam electrolysis offers a promising route to lower electrical energy requirements where suitable heat integration is available. None of this automatically makes nuclear hydrogen economical: diverted electricity has an opportunity cost, and electrolysers degrade, need substantial capital and can lose to other hydrogen pathways.

Hydrox combines a physics-based hydrogen process model with constrained optimisation of electrical allocation, production scheduling, equipment degradation and emissions. A 2026 Barakah-specific study estimated HTSE hydrogen at approximately $3.39/kg under its assumptions; Hydrox uses independent evidence like this as a realistic baseline, then investigates whether better operating strategies can improve on it.

SHEET 12 / FOUNDERS
Founders' note

Why we're building Hydrox.

Barakah already produces large amounts of firm, low-carbon electricity. We kept asking whether some of it could be worth more as hydrogen, and found the honest answer is "it depends": on electricity value, hydrogen demand, equipment degradation and grid emissions. Hydrox is our attempt to quantify that with a model that is open about its assumptions, stays outside reactor safety systems, and is willing to conclude that exporting the electricity is the better choice.

We're at the concept and simulation stage, and there is a lot we don't know yet. If you work in nuclear, process engineering or controls and can help us find out, we'd like to hear from you.
